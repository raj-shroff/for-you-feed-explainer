# Final five-screen teaching outline

1. **How TikTok picks your next video.** Introduces the purpose and invites learners to experiment.
2. **Your actions leave clues.** Watch, like, or skip either video. Selections are reversible and persist into the sorting screen. The exact requested line is: Your choices teach TikTok what you might like.
3. **Which video might you watch next?** Sort using the previous choices. Change my choices returns to screen 2, allowing the class to compare outcomes.
4. **TikTok shows you new content, to see if you like it.** Add a space video and decide whether to watch, like, or skip it. The response helps initialize screen 5.
5. **Try changing your interests.** Pick cooking, basketball, or space; watch, like, or skip; observe the suggestion cards change beside the current video. The recent-choice strip shows the actions behind the changes. Reset choices clears this experiment.

## Teaching model

The five screens explain documented signal types and feedback using a small scripted example. They do not reproduce TikTok's weights or timing. Sorting groups likes, watches, unexplored topics, and skips for visible teaching purposes; this particular order is not a claim about real relative signal weights. The final screen moves the most recently watched or liked topic forward and demotes skipped topics. Repeated images stand for different fictional videos about a subject.

Sources S1–S4 support the core explanations. The broader research catalog retains S5–S6 for background, but Monolith and its technical claims have been removed from the animation. The final screen demonstrates how fresh choices can change recommendations without making a speed comparison against competing apps.

## Presentation

Use the interactive controls rather than reading a description of a feedback loop. Try liking basketball and skipping cooking; sort; then reverse those choices. On the final screen, watch space, then like cooking, then skip cooking to make the change visible.
